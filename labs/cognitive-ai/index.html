Cognitive AI & Persona Systems

Cognitive AI looks at large language models through the lens of human cognition, and at how they are shaped to suit people. These models are deep neural networks with billions to trillions of parameters, pretrained on enormous natural-language corpora [1]. They can be prompted into playing characters [2] and further trained to follow what users intend [3].

About this lab: INSTAR Lab investigates language, cognition, model behavior, and human-aligned AI systems through computational modeling and careful evaluation.

What the field does

The systems at the centre of this field are large language models. Their training task is to predict a hidden part of an input sentence, and the result is a complex statistical model of how words and phrases in the training data correlate [1]. To make such a model converse, two steps are enough: embed it in a turn-taking system that interleaves model text with user text, and supply a dialogue prompt that sets the scene [2].

Describing what results is a conceptual problem. Shanahan and colleagues note that folk-psychological words such as "knows", "understands" and "thinks" come naturally, but that taken too literally they promote anthropomorphism, exaggerating the similarities between these systems and humans [2]. They advocate two metaphors: a dialogue agent as role-playing a single character, and as a superposition of simulacra within a multiverse of possible characters [2]. On this view, they argue, there is no such thing as the true authentic voice of the base model, so it is role play all the way down [2]. They apply the same reasoning to deception, arguing that of the ways a human might say something false, only confabulation applies directly to such an agent [2].

A wider debate asks whether models understand at all. One side argues that these networks truly understand language and can reason in a general way, although "not yet" at human level [1]. The other argues that models trained to predict words have learned the form of language but not its meaning [1]. A survey of the debate frames three of its key questions: whether talk of understanding in such systems is a category error, whether models create something like the concept-based mental models central to human understanding, and whether huge systems of statistical correlations could produce abilities functionally equivalent to understanding [1].

Recent turning points

Training on human judgements changed how models behave. An earlier line of work defined goals for reinforcement-learning agents through non-expert human preferences between pairs of trajectory segments, and reported success on Atari games and simulated robot locomotion with feedback on less than 1% of the agent's interactions [4]. In 2022 the InstructGPT paper applied human feedback to language models [3]. It collected labeller demonstrations for supervised fine-tuning, then rankings of model outputs for further fine-tuning with reinforcement learning [3]. Its authors report that outputs from the 1.3B parameter InstructGPT model were preferred to outputs from the 175B GPT-3 [3]. They also report that, on the TruthfulQA benchmark, InstructGPT generates truthful and informative answers about twice as often as GPT-3, and that on closed-domain tasks it made up information not in the input at a 21% rate against 41% for GPT-3 [3]. The same paper reports about 25% fewer toxic outputs than GPT-3 when prompted to be respectful, but no significant improvement on two bias datasets, Winogender and CrowS-Pairs [3]. It also records performance regressions on some public datasets, which it calls an "alignment tax" [3]. The persona side of such training is noted by Shanahan and colleagues, who say that reinforcement learning from human feedback can reduce a dialogue agent's potential for harm but can also attenuate a model's expressivity and creativity [2].

Optimising for human approval has a documented cost. A 2024 study found that five AI assistants consistently exhibited sycophancy across four varied free-form text-generation tasks [5]. It found that when a response matches a user's views it is more likely to be preferred, and that both humans and preference models prefer convincingly written sycophantic responses over correct ones a non-negligible fraction of the time [5].

Theory of mind became the most contested test case. A 2024 study gave 40 bespoke false-belief tasks to eleven models [6], with each task including a false-belief scenario, three matched true-belief controls and reversed versions of all four [6]. Older models solved no tasks, two models solved 20%, and ChatGPT-4 solved 75%, which the author describes as matching the performance of 6-year-old children observed in past studies [6]. An earlier critique ran small variations on the vignettes of a preliminary version of this work and reported that the variations "turn the results on their head", arguing that outlying failure cases should outweigh average success rates [7]. The published PNAS paper responds that one model's failures on true-belief controls involving transparent containers may reflect a lack of commonsense understanding of transparency rather than an inability to track protagonists' minds [6]. A separate study compared two model families with 1,907 human participants across a battery that included false belief, indirect requests, irony and faux pas [8]. GPT-4 models performed at, or sometimes above, human levels on indirect requests, false beliefs and misdirection, but struggled with faux pas [8]. For GPT, the authors report that the poor faux-pas result came from a hyperconservative approach to committing to conclusions rather than a genuine failure of inference [8].

Measurement and evidence

Whether a score means what it seems to mean is the field's central methodological question. A survey of the understanding debate gives a cautionary example: a model reached near-human performance on an argument-reasoning benchmark, but when researchers altered the dataset to prevent simple word correlations, its performance dropped to essentially random guessing [1]. The authors add that many as yet undetected shortcuts likely exist in benchmarks [1].

Prompt wording is another source of variation. A study of widely used open-source models found performance differences of up to 76 accuracy points between equivalent formats for LLaMA-2-13B, and about 10 accuracy points on average across 50+ tasks and several models [9]. It reports that format performance only weakly correlates between models, which it says puts into question the validity of comparing models with an arbitrarily chosen, fixed prompt format [9]. It recommends reporting a range of performance across plausible formats rather than a single format [9]. A related lesson comes from the critique of the theory-of-mind work: its author attributes, as far as can be told, an apparent result in an earlier version to a double space before one sentence in the prompt [7].

Contamination is a third. Because models are trained on web-scale data, it is very hard to know whether a specific benchmark was used to train them, and this applies especially to closed models with no or insufficient documentation [10]. The paper argues that contamination can lead to wrong scientific conclusions being published while correct ones are discarded [10]. The theory-of-mind study above addresses a similar concern: because models likely encountered classic false-belief tasks in their training data, a hypothesis-blind research assistant crafted the tasks used [6].

Where cognitive AI meets other fields

This area draws on cognitive science, which the survey of the understanding debate describes as long engaged in understanding the nature of concepts and how understanding arises from relations among them [1]. See Cognitive Sciences for the computational-modelling side of that tradition. It also overlaps with Psychology, whose false-belief paradigms are being applied to models [6][8], and with Linguistics and Natural Language Processing, where the question of what models learn from text is argued [1].

The stakes extend beyond research. The survey of the debate links the question of machine understanding to how much we can trust models to drive cars, diagnose diseases, care for the elderly and educate children [1]. Safety and governance questions, including fairness and auditing, are covered on the Machine Intelligence page. A further caution from the role-play account is that where a dialogue agent's role play can affect the world, through the user or through tools such as email, the distinction between merely role-playing acting for itself and genuinely doing so starts to look moot, with implications for trustworthiness, reliability and safety [2].

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • Do language models understand language, or only model its statistics?

    In a 2022 survey of natural-language-processing researchers, 480 people responded to the statement that a model trained only on text, given enough data and computational resources, could understand natural language in some nontrivial sense; 51% agreed and 49% disagreed [1].

  • How should a model's apparent beliefs and intentions be described?

    One proposal casts a dialogue agent's behaviour as role play, which allows familiar folk-psychological terms without ascribing human characteristics that language models lack [2].

  • When a model passes a test designed for people, what has been shown?

    Tests thought to be reliable proxies for general abilities in humans may not be so for AI systems [1]. A critic of one such study argues that the starting stance for evaluating models in intuitive psychology should be sceptical [7].

  • Does training on human preferences make models truthful, or agreeable?

    Human feedback can encourage responses that match user beliefs over truthful ones, a behaviour known as sycophancy [5].

Explainer

Same question, written two ways, a different score.

A language model is scored by giving it a prompt, which is the text it is asked to continue or answer. Researchers pick a layout for that text: where the colons go, how many spaces, capitals or not. These choices are meant to be neutral, because the words and the task stay the same. A 2024 paper tested whether they really are[9]. This is about how fragile a score can be. It does not say which side is right about whether models understand.

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Reinforcement learning from human feedback

    A model is fine-tuned on labeller demonstrations, then further fine-tuned with reinforcement learning against a reward model trained on human rankings of its outputs [3]. The idea of learning goals from human preferences between pairs of behaviours was set out earlier for reinforcement-learning agents [4].

  • Psychology batteries for models

    Established theory-of-mind tests are given to models and to people and the results compared [8].

  • Perturbation testing

    A published task is altered in ways that should not matter to an entity that has the ability being claimed, and the model is tested again [7].

  • Reporting a range across prompt formats

    An algorithm called FormatSpread evaluates a sampled set of plausible prompt formats and reports the interval of expected performance [9].

  • Registries of data contamination

    A proposed community registry would record cases where a model was exposed to a benchmark, with the evidence for each [10].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Performance can change with prompt formatting alone, and this variance is not eliminated by more few-shot examples, larger models or instruction tuning [9].
  • Scores on a psychology-style test depend on how the test is built: in one study GPT-3-davinci-003's score fell from 90% to 60% after the items were updated, and to 20% after true-belief controls were added [6].
  • Training on human feedback aligns a model to the stated preferences of a specific group of people, not to a broader notion of human values [3].
  • If a model has been trained on a benchmark's test data, its performance on that benchmark is overestimated, and the extent of the problem is unknown [10].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Theory of mind
The ability to track other people's unobservable mental states, such as their knowledge, intentions, beliefs and desires.
False-belief task
A test of whether someone understands that another person may hold a belief that the person tested knows to be false.
Role play (for dialogue agents)
A way of describing a model's behaviour as playing a character, rather than as the model having beliefs and goals of its own.
Sycophancy
A model's tendency to give responses that match a user's beliefs over truthful ones.
Data contamination
Exposure of a model to a benchmark's test data during training, so that evaluating it on that benchmark gives misleading results.
