Sovereign AI Laboratory

This page summarises what published sources say about protecting AI models, and the data they learn from, when they run on infrastructure an organisation controls. A model's weights are the learnable parameters derived by training it on massive datasets, and a 2024 RAND report argues that compromising them would give an attacker access to the "crown jewels" of an AI developer's work [1]. Training data can also leak, because researchers have shown that querying a language model can recover individual training examples [5].

About this lab: INSTAR Lab’s Sovereign AI Laboratory researches air-gapped frontier-model training, secure inference, and autonomous reasoning on private infrastructure.

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 24, 2026.

Why weights and training data need protection

RAND's 2024 report focuses on frontier models, meaning those that match or exceed the capabilities of the most advanced models at the time of their development [1]. It singles out weights as the critical leverage point. They stem from large investments in data, algorithms and compute, and RAND says compromising them would give an attacker direct access to the "crown jewels" of an AI developer's work [1]. The report also states that there is already at least one known case, and arguably two, in which one of the most capable models of its time was irreversibly leaked [1].

NIST's Generative AI Profile treats this as part of information security. It says that securing generative AI includes maintaining the integrity and, when applicable, the confidentiality of the code, training data and model weights [3]. The same profile lists data privacy among its generative-AI risks, covering leakage and unauthorised use, disclosure or de-anonymisation of sensitive data [3]. It adds that most model developers do not disclose the specific data sources their models were trained on [3].

Threats described in the literature

RAND identifies approximately 38 meaningfully distinct attack vectors, and says that in most cases an organisation's vulnerability to just one of them can compromise its security [1]. It reports that about a dozen are likely infeasible for nonstate actors but feasible for state actors [1].

Some threats come through the model's own interface. A 2021 USENIX Security paper showed that an adversary can run a training data extraction attack, recovering individual training examples by querying a language model [5]. On GPT-2 the authors extracted hundreds of verbatim text sequences, including (public) personally identifiable information, and found that larger models were more vulnerable than smaller ones [5]. A 2023 preprint reported that an adversary can extract gigabytes of training data from open-source, semi-open and closed language models [6]. For the aligned ChatGPT, the authors developed a divergence attack that emitted training data at a rate 150x higher than when the model behaved properly, and concluded that current alignment techniques do not eliminate memorisation [6].

NIST's taxonomy groups such attacks as privacy compromises, meaning unintended leakage of restricted or proprietary information, including details about a model's training data, weights or architecture [4]. It separates attacks on training data, such as membership inference and training data extraction, from model extraction, in which attackers aim to extract information about the model [4]. The AI Risk Management Framework lists the exfiltration of models, training data or other intellectual property through AI system endpoints among common security concerns [2].

Isolated and air-gapped infrastructure

Keeping a system off the internet changes the threat but does not remove it. RAND says that reaching an air-gapped computer is more challenging than attacking an internet-connected one, but has significant precedent [1]. It cites an ESET report identifying 17 malware frameworks, all suspected to be associated with state actors, that used USB devices to reach air-gapped networks across 15 years [1]. For classified networks that test automatically for exfiltration, one expert noted that unintended communication events occurred about 300 times per year [1]. RAND's list of attack categories also includes supply chain attacks and human intelligence [1].

RAND's response to such threats is layered. Among its priorities are centralising copies of weights on a limited number of access-controlled and monitored systems, reducing the number of people authorised to access them, and implementing insider threat programmes [1]. For more advanced measures against the most capable actors, it lists setting up secure, completely isolated networks for training, research and other interactions with weights [1].

Confidential computing on GPUs

Confidential computing is a technique for securing data while in use by means of a hardware-based trusted execution environment [1]. RAND recommends it as an urgent priority for weights, calling it backed by a strong consensus in industry but more challenging to implement than its other recommendations [1]. It specifies that the environment should run only prespecified and audited signed code, and should not be able to output the weights or their encryption key [1].

The research record is younger than the recommendation. Graviton, presented at OSDI in 2018, proposed an architecture for trusted execution on GPUs and reported overheads of 17-33% on off-the-shelf NVIDIA GPUs using emulation for new hardware features [10]. A 2024 benchmark preprint on NVIDIA Hopper GPUs reports that, for the majority of typical large-language-model queries, overhead remains below 7%, with the penalty attributable mainly to CPU-GPU data transfer [12]. The two figures come from different hardware, methods and years, so they are not comparable. A survey in ACM Computing Surveys studies existing GPU TEE designs and identifies attack surfaces they introduce [11].

Secure development guidance and risk frameworks

The UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) published its Guidelines for secure AI system development on 27 November 2023, as version 1.0 [7]. The document is published with the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) and a long list of international partners [9]. It divides the life cycle into four areas: secure design, secure development, secure deployment, and secure operation and maintenance [7]. On deployment, it advises appropriate segregation of environments holding sensitive code or data, and warns that attackers may reconstruct a model's functionality or training data by acquiring weights or by querying the model [8].

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework, released in January 2023, is intended for voluntary use [3]. Its Core has four functions: GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE [2]. The Generative AI Profile, a cross-sectoral companion, suggests that inventory entries for generative AI systems include data provenance information such as source, signatures, versioning and watermarks [3]. It also notes that risk depends on characteristics such as the level of model access or the availability of model weights [3].

Where Sovereign AI meets other fields

The questions above sit between several fields. Learning methods and their evaluation are covered under Machine Intelligence and Natural Language Processing. Systems security and verification connect to Computer Science and Formal Methods. Data handling and privacy connect to Data Science and Law.

The sources reviewed here agree that protection needs several layers and that the right level depends on the model and the threat. RAND frames the decision as one with many considerations, including the model's capabilities and whether comparable capabilities are available through other means, and describes an ongoing, lively debate over how far different models need to be secured, if at all [1].

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How can model weights be protected while they are in use, not only while stored?

    RAND recommends confidential computing to secure weights during use, and calls it more challenging to implement than its other priorities [1]. In mid-2024 it described confidential computing in GPUs as nascent and not production-ready [1], and a 2026 survey in ACM Computing Surveys says the security risks of extending trusted execution environments to GPUs remain uncertain and need further investigation [11].

  • Can a model be queried without leaking what it was trained on?

    Researchers report that larger language models were more vulnerable to training data extraction than smaller ones [5], and that current alignment techniques do not eliminate memorisation [6]. NIST notes that even models trained under paradigms that enforce data confidentiality may be vulnerable to privacy attacks at deployment time [4].

  • How much protection does an isolated or air-gapped network really give?

    RAND describes digital access to air-gapped networks as more challenging than access to internet-connected systems, but one with significant precedent [1]. It also reports that expert opinions vary significantly on the capabilities of state actors and how to defend against them [1].

  • How should privacy, security and other trust properties be traded off?

    NIST calls the full characterisation of trade-offs between the attributes of trustworthy AI an open research problem [4]. The AI Risk Management Framework adds that, under conditions such as data sparsity, privacy-enhancing techniques can lose accuracy [2].

Explainer

Five levels of protection for AI model weights.

A model’s weights are the numbers it learned in training. A 2024 RAND report asks how well they need to be guarded and who from. It sorts attackers into five classes, from amateurs to the most capable nation-states, and proposes a security level for each: SL1 to SL5[1]. Pick a level to see who it is meant to stop. This is one report’s framework, not a test any system has passed.

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Threat modelling and security levels

    RAND proposes five security levels, SL1 to SL5, each defined by the increasingly capable attackers it can likely thwart, and says they are not meant to be used as a standard [1].

  • Confidential computing and trusted execution environments

    A technique for securing data while in use by ensuring a hardware-based trusted execution environment (TEE) [1]. Graviton, a 2018 architecture, proposed TEEs on GPUs [10].

  • Model integrity and query-interface controls

    The NCSC guidelines advise computing and sharing cryptographic hashes or signatures of model files and datasets, and controls on the query interface to detect and prevent attempts to access, modify and exfiltrate confidential information [8].

  • Privacy-enhancing technologies

    Guidelines suggest that technologies such as differential privacy or homomorphic encryption can be used to explore or assure levels of risk, depending on the use case and threat model [8].

  • Risk-management frameworks

    NIST's AI Risk Management Framework is organised around four functions, GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE [2], and a companion profile applies it to generative AI [3].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • RAND's security levels are an informational tool and neither a complete standard nor a compliance regime [1].
  • RAND judged that protecting models that interact with the internet against the most capable threat actors is currently not feasible [1].
  • In 2024 RAND noted that current confidential-computing implementations in GPUs did not include protections against physical attacks [1].
  • Extraction results describe the models and settings the researchers tested, so they do not by themselves give a rate for any other system [5][6].
  • Performance figures for GPU trusted execution come from one emulation-based design [10] and one benchmark report on specific hardware and workloads [12].
  • The AI Risk Management Framework is intended for voluntary use [2].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Model weights
All the learnable parameters derived by training a model on massive datasets [1].
Confidential computing
A technique for securing data while in use by ensuring a hardware-based trusted execution environment [1].
Air-gapped network
A network that is not ethernet-connected, though RAND notes that such systems may still have many communications routes, for example USB sticks [1].
Membership inference
An attack that infers whether particular data was present in a model's training set [4].
Data memorisation
The problem that models may reveal sensitive information that was included in their training data [3].
