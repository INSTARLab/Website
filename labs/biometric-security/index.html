Biometrics & Drone Sensing

Biometrics is the automated recognition of individuals from biological and behavioural characteristics, such as facial features, voice patterns, typing speed or gait [2]. Ordinary cameras can also pick up a pulse from the skin [4], and small uncrewed aircraft carry sensors for remote sensing [7]. These uses raise shared questions about accuracy, fairness, spoofing and rules.

About this lab: INSTAR Lab researches advanced biometric sensing on phones and laptops without specialized hardware, and drone sensing developed with Ravonics LLC.

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 24, 2026.

What the field covers

NIST's digital identity guidelines define biometrics as the automated recognition of individuals based on their biological and behavioural characteristics, such as fingerprints, voice patterns, facial features, keystroke patterns, the angle of holding a smart phone, screen pressure, typing speed, mouse movements or gait [2]. They name a camera as one kind of biometric sensor [2].

A comparison is never exact. The measurement is subject to noise and presentation variations, so a threshold must be set. Some comparisons then fail to match when they should (the false non-match rate), and some impostor comparisons match (the false match rate) [2]. NIST notes that the threshold usually emphasises a low false match rate for security, because false non-matches can often be fixed by repeating the measurement or using another method [2].

Face recognition and demographic differences

A major public test of demographic effects in face recognition is NIST's NISTIR 8280, published in December 2019 [1]. Its authors quantified accuracy for groups defined by sex, age, and race or country of birth, using one-to-one verification and one-to-many identification algorithms [1]. They processed a total of 18.27 million images of 8.49 million people through 189 mostly commercial algorithms from 99 developers [1]. NIST says the algorithms were prototypes, not necessarily available as mature integrable products [1].

NIST's summary states: "Contemporary face recognition algorithms exhibit demographic differentials of various magnitudes." [1] Its main result was that false positive differentials are much larger than those related to false negatives, and exist broadly, across many, but not all, algorithms tested [1]. NIST reports that "false positives rates often vary by factors of 10 to beyond 100 times" across demographics, while false negatives "tend to be more algorithm-specific, and vary often by factors below 3" [1].

Results depend on the algorithm and the images. There is a wide range in accuracy across developers, and the most accurate algorithms can be expected to have smaller demographic differentials [1]. With a number of algorithms developed in China, the effect for East Asian faces was reversed, with low false positive rates [1].

NIST's practical conclusion is: "it is incumbent upon the system owner to know their algorithm" [1]. It says it will usually be informative to measure accuracy of the operational algorithm on the operational image data [1].

Authentication standards reflect these concerns. SP 800-63B-4 says a biometric system shall operate with a false match rate of 1 in 10,000 or better for all demographic groups, and that the categories considered shall include sex and skin tone when these factors affect performance [2]. The system should show a false non-match rate of less than 5%, and shall use a fixed threshold, because it is not feasible to change the threshold for each demographic [2].

Spoofing and presentation attacks

Biometric characteristics are not secrets. NIST notes that they can often be obtained online or otherwise without consent, and that a facial image can be obtained by taking a picture [2]. Presentation attack detection (PAD) can mitigate these risks, but additional trust in the sensor or biometric processing is required to ensure PAD is operating as intended [2].

A survey of deep-learning face anti-spoofing describes its aim as securing face recognition systems from presentation attacks [3]. Its authors report that deep-learning methods now dominate the area, while handcrafted methods became unreliable as more realistic attacks of novel types appeared [3].

SP 800-63B-4 sets specific rules. Biometrics shall only be used as part of multi-factor authentication with a physical authenticator, and a non-biometric option shall always be provided [2]. The system shall implement PAD for facial recognition, and biometric comparison based on voice shall not be used [2]. Deployment testing should demonstrate an impostor attack presentation accept rate of less than 0.07 [2].

Behavioural signals

Behavioural biometrics use how a person acts rather than how they look. NIST lists keystroke patterns, the angle of holding a smart phone, screen pressure, typing speed, mouse movements and gait among biometric characteristics [2].

Heart rate from a camera

Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) measures a pulse from small colour changes in skin. A 2008 study measured plethysmographic signals remotely (more than 1 m) using ambient light and a simple consumer-level digital camera in movie mode [4]. Heart and respiration rates could be quantified up to several harmonics [4]. A 2021 review describes how deep-learning methods are applied to remote heart rate measurement [6].

A 2021 study in npj Digital Medicine collected pulse rate and facial video in India and Sierra Leone and compared state-of-the-art rPPG methods with a contact-based device [5]. Their data covered 140 subjects, 44 from India and 96 from Sierra Leone [5]. They report that rPPG methods exhibit similar biases compared with the contact device across demographic groups and environmental conditions [5]. The mean difference from the ground truth was about 2% (1 beats per minute), while pulse rate variability was about 15% (11 beats per minute) [5]. The mean errors of the five individual methods in their Table 4 ranged from -14.11 to 14.92 beats per minute against the reference device, so a small average can hide larger errors in particular methods [5].

Sensing from small drones, and the rules

A 2019 review states that sensors and platforms on uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs) are used in almost every application that needs observed information from the top or oblique views, such as agriculture, forestry and mining [7]. It also notes that ultra-high-resolution data, with a ground sampling distance of less than 10 cm, bring more unwanted classes of objects, such as pedestrians and cars, into land-cover classification [7].

In the United States, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) describes its Part 107 rules as covering commercial and government uses of drones weighing less than 55 pounds [8]. The operator must keep the drone within sight and must not fly over people unless they are directly participating in the operation [8]. The FAA page says the maximum allowable altitude is 400 feet above the ground, higher if the drone remains within 400 feet of a structure, and the maximum speed is 100 mph (87 knots) [8]. Part 107 pilots must register each drone, and operating the controls requires a remote pilot certificate with a small UAS rating or direct supervision by a certificate holder [8]. The page presents highlights of the rule, 14 CFR Part 107, so readers who need the exact requirements should consult the regulation itself [8].

Remote ID is a separate requirement. The FAA defines it as the ability of a drone in flight to provide identification and location information that can be received by other parties through a broadcast signal [9]. Drones that are required to be registered, or are registered, must comply, through a Standard Remote ID drone, a Remote ID broadcast module, or flying within an FAA-Recognized Identification Area [9].

Where biometrics and drone sensing meet other fields

Fairness testing of face recognition connects to the wider study of bias in learning systems; see Machine Intelligence and Data Science. Spoofing and authentication are security problems within Computer Science. Camera-based pulse measurement draws on cardiovascular measurement, discussed in Physiology. Rules on identification, privacy and aviation sit within Law, and questions about who is affected connect to Sociology.

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How large are the accuracy differences between demographic groups, and can they be reduced?

    NIST found that face recognition algorithms exhibit demographic differentials of various magnitudes, and it recommended research into mitigation [1]. How far mitigation works in operational systems is still being tested.

  • How can a face-based system resist spoofing that keeps changing?

    A survey of deep-learning face anti-spoofing notes that more realistic presentation attacks of novel types keep appearing, and that earlier handcrafted methods became unreliable as they did [3]. Open issues are listed in the survey itself [3].

  • Does camera-based pulse measurement work equally well across skin tones, settings and methods?

    A field study of 140 people in India and Sierra Leone reported similar biases across demographic groups and conditions, but wide spread in individual readings and in the errors of individual methods [5].

  • How should sensing from small drones be matched to rules on safety and identification?

    A review of UAV remote sensing reports that ultra-high-resolution data bring more unwanted classes of objects, such as pedestrians and cars, into land-cover classification [7]. The United States has separate rules for how small drones may be flown and identified [8][9].

Explainer

An average can hide large errors.

An ordinary camera can pick up a pulse from tiny colour changes in the skin of a face. Several computer methods do this. A 2021 study tested five of them on facial video from 140 people in India and Sierra Leone, and checked each reading against a finger pulse oximeter, a clip that measures pulse from the fingertip[5]. These are results for those methods on that video, not advice about any app or device.

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Biometric comparison against a threshold

    A sensor measurement is compared with a reference, and a threshold decides whether it counts as a match; the choice trades false matches against false non-matches [2].

  • Demographic testing of algorithms

    Large sets of photographs with sex, age and country-of-birth information are run through many algorithms to measure error rates by group [1].

  • Presentation attack detection

    Methods that tell a live face from a spoof, including deep-learning methods trained with pixel-wise supervision such as pseudo depth maps [3].

  • Remote photoplethysmography (rPPG)

    Pulse and breathing rates are estimated from subtle colour changes in video of the skin [4][6].

  • UAV remote sensing

    Sensors on small uncrewed aircraft collect images and derived products such as digital surface models and orthophotos [7].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • NIST's demographic results describe the algorithms and photograph sets it tested, which it says were prototypes and not necessarily mature products, so they do not describe every system [1].
  • Biometric comparison is probabilistic, so a match is a degree of confidence and not a certainty [2].
  • Reported rPPG averages can look close to a contact reference while individual readings vary widely [5].
  • A review of UAV remote sensing found that processing and analysis methods are still largely ad hoc to applications [7].
  • The official pages cited here should be checked for the current text of the rules.

Terms used on this page

Glossary

False match rate (FMR)
The probability that an impostor comparison results in a match [2].
False non-match rate (FNMR)
The probability that a comparison does not result in a match [2].
Presentation attack
A spoofing attempt against a face recognition system [3].
Remote photoplethysmography
Measuring pulse and breathing rates remotely from light changes in the skin with a camera [4].
Remote ID
The ability of a drone in flight to provide identification and location information that other parties can receive through a broadcast signal [9].
