INSTAR Lab · Research Brief · July 2026

Law Written for One Pace of Change, Applied to Another

Legal and regulatory systems were built to govern technologies that changed over years or decades. Artificial intelligence, biotechnology, and quantum computing are moving on a shorter timescale, and the resulting gap between the speed of innovation and the speed of legal doctrine is itself a research problem worth studying directly.

Regulatory Lag

Law Moving at the Speed of Statute, Technology Moving Faster

Regulatory systems are, by design, deliberate: rulemaking processes involve notice-and-comment periods, legislative debate, and judicial review precisely because binding public rules should not change on a whim. That deliberateness is a feature when the underlying subject matter is stable. It becomes a structural mismatch when the subject matter — a large language model's capabilities, a gene-editing technique's precision, a quantum algorithm's cryptographic implications — can shift meaningfully within the time it takes a single rulemaking cycle to complete.

This mismatch shows up across each of these domains in a similar shape: existing legal categories were built around assumptions that fast-moving technology strains. Liability doctrine assumes a identifiable human decision-maker whose negligence or intent can be evaluated; systems with autonomous or emergent behavior complicate that assumption without necessarily invalidating the underlying legal concept. Export control and biosecurity frameworks assume that dangerous capability is tied to specialized, hard-to-acquire equipment; software and increasingly accessible laboratory techniques weaken that assumption in ways regulators are still working through. None of this means existing law is irrelevant — it means the research question of how to adapt it, rather than start over, is genuinely open.

Existing Frameworks

Existing Frameworks as Building Blocks

A recurring finding in science and technology law scholarship is that most emerging-technology governance problems are not, in fact, unprecedented — administrative law, products liability, intellectual property doctrine, and sector-specific regulatory regimes each carry decades of accumulated reasoning about how to handle uncertainty, allocate responsibility, and balance innovation against public risk. The research task is identifying which parts of that accumulated reasoning transfer cleanly to a new technology, which parts require deliberate extension, and which parts genuinely break down and need something closer to a new doctrine.

Doing that analysis rigorously requires working directly with the primary legal materials — statutes, regulations, case law, and legislative history — at a scale that makes systematic, cross-jurisdictional comparison possible rather than relying on a handful of illustrative examples. That is a natural point of intersection between legal scholarship and computational methods: the same techniques used to process large text corpora in other fields can surface patterns across thousands of regulatory documents or judicial opinions that would be impractical to identify by manual review alone, without replacing the legal judgment required to interpret what those patterns mean.

INSTAR Perspective

How INSTAR Approaches Science and Technology Law

INSTAR's law research program examines how regulatory systems can keep pace with artificial intelligence, gene editing, autonomous systems, and quantum technologies — analyzing the structural gaps between the speed of innovation and the evolution of legal doctrine, and developing framework analyses that aim to protect the public while preserving space for beneficial research. This work combines legal scholarship with machine-learning-assisted review of regulatory corpora, letting us surface patterns across jurisdictions at a scale traditional legal research methods struggle to match.

As with our other research programs, this analysis is grounded in openly accessible legal materials — digitized case law, authenticated federal statutes and regulations, and legislative records — so that our conclusions can be checked against the same primary sources any other researcher could consult. Because this program sits at the intersection of law and INSTAR's broader technical work in AI, biotechnology-adjacent life sciences, and quantum computing, it is naturally interdisciplinary: legal scholars, computer scientists, and domain researchers working through the same governance questions from different angles. Researchers interested in science and technology law are welcome to explore the INSTAR law program or connect via community/contact.
