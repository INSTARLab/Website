Technology / Field overview

Computer Science

Computer science studies what can be computed, how efficiently, and how to build reliable systems that do it. Its theory sets limits on what any machine can do, and its practice shapes nearly every other field of research [1][7].

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 23, 2026.

What computer science is

Computer science asks what can be computed at all, how much time and memory computation needs, and how to build systems that do it dependably. Alan Turing's 1937 paper on computable numbers, published in the Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society, addressed the Entscheidungsproblem, the logicians' "decision problem" [1]. It is an early example of treating computation as an object of mathematical study.

Theory matters in practice because it tells engineers what to expect. A problem can be solvable in principle but too slow to solve at scale. How running time grows with input size is therefore a central question, and Shor's work on factoring, discussed below, turns on exactly that distinction [2].

Recent turning points

Quantum computing is the clearest recent example of theory prompting experiment. In 1997, Peter Shor published efficient randomized algorithms for factoring integers and finding discrete logarithms on a hypothetical quantum computer. They take a number of steps polynomial in the input size [2]. These two problems are generally thought hard for classical computers and underpin several proposed cryptosystems [2].

In 2019, Arute and colleagues published an experiment with a programmable superconducting processor, under the heading of quantum supremacy [4]. Such claims concern a quantum device performing a particular task beyond the reach of classical computers. They are statements about one task, not about computers in general.

John Preskill's 2018 paper takes a measured view of the near term. It describes Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) technology. Quantum computers with 50 to 100 qubits, he wrote, may perform tasks that surpass the capabilities of today's classical digital computers, but noise in quantum gates limits the size of circuits that can be executed reliably [3]. In his words, the 100-qubit quantum computer will not change the world right away; it is a significant step toward more powerful quantum technologies [3].

How results are checked

Computer science relies on mathematical proof for some claims and on experiments and benchmarks for others. Both need care.

For computational experiments, one proposed standard is reproducibility. Roger Peng's 2011 article in Science noted that the nature of computational work exposes limitations in our ability to evaluate published findings, and proposed reproducibility as a minimum standard when full independent replication of a study is not possible [5].

Privacy is a place where computer science supplies precise definitions. The monograph on the algorithmic foundations of differential privacy presents a rigorous definition and a class of algorithms that satisfy it, motivated by the growing detail of data about individuals [6].

Where computer science meets other fields

Computer science supplies the machinery behind data science and machine intelligence. Machine learning itself is described as lying at the intersection of computer science and statistics, with recent progress driven by new algorithms and theory and by the growth of online data and low-cost computation [7]. Quantum computing links computer science to physics and engineering [3]. Privacy research links it to statistics and law [6].

The National Artificial Intelligence Research Resource (NAIRR) Pilot illustrates the infrastructure side. It states its aim as connecting United States researchers and educators to computational, data and training resources [8].

For funders and research partners, the sound posture is patience with long timelines. Shor's 1997 algorithms and Arute and colleagues' 2019 experiment are separated by more than twenty years [2][4], and Preskill's 2018 assessment suggests that further waiting may be needed [3]. Early claims of advantage deserve careful reading.

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How large must a quantum computer be, and how accurate, before it is useful for practical problems?

    Preskill argued that devices with 50 to 100 qubits may surpass today's classical computers on some tasks, but noise in quantum gates limits how large a circuit can reliably run [3].

  • How can computational results be made reproducible?

    Reproducibility has been proposed as a minimum standard for judging computational claims when full independent replication is not possible [5].

  • How can useful analysis of personal data be reconciled with privacy?

    Differential privacy gives a rigorous definition of privacy, and research continues on its practical costs [6].

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Theory of computation

    Mathematical models of what machines can and cannot compute [1].

  • Algorithm design and analysis

    Creating step-by-step procedures and proving how their running time grows with input size [2].

  • Quantum algorithms and hardware

    Algorithms for quantum computers, and experimental processors built to run them [2][4].

  • Privacy-preserving computation

    Algorithms that satisfy a formal privacy definition [6].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Quantum devices today are noisy, and noise limits the size of circuits that can run reliably [3].
  • Claims of quantum advantage apply to specific tasks, not to computing in general [4][3].
  • Reproducibility of computational work cannot be assumed from publication alone [5].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Algorithm
A precise, step-by-step procedure for solving a problem.
Qubit
The basic unit of quantum information, the quantum counterpart of a bit.
Polynomial time
A running time that grows as a fixed power of the input size, usually seen as efficient.
Entscheidungsproblem
The 'decision problem' in logic, asking for a general method to decide whether a statement is provable.
