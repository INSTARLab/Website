Technology / Field overview

Natural Language Processing

Natural language processing (NLP) is the study of how computers read, interpret and generate human language. Recent systems can translate, answer questions and write fluent text [1][3]. Judging whether that fluency is reliable is a separate and harder problem, and it is where much current research is focused [4][6].

What natural language processing is

Natural language processing (NLP) covers tasks such as translation, question answering and summarisation. Before 2017, the leading sequence models were built on complex recurrent or convolutional neural networks [1]. In 2017, a research team proposed a simpler design, the Transformer, built solely on attention mechanisms and dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely [1].

The authors reported that on a standard 2014 English-to-German translation benchmark, their model reached 28.4 on the BLEU score (a standard measure of translation quality), and that on English-to-French it set a new single-model best of 41.8 after 3.5 days of training on eight graphics processors [1]. They also stressed that the design was more parallelizable and cheaper to train than earlier approaches [1].

Recent turning points

Two ideas built on the Transformer. The first is pre-training: a model learns general properties of language from a large body of text and is then adapted to a task. The BERT model (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) is a well-known example, presented at a 2019 conference of the North American chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics [2].

The second is scale. In 2020, researchers described GPT-3, an autoregressive language model with 175 billion parameters, which they said was ten times more than any previous non-sparse language model [3]. They tested it in a few-shot setting, with tasks and examples given purely as text and without further training of the model [3]. They reported strong performance on many datasets, and sometimes competitiveness with earlier fine-tuned systems [3].

Before this, methods typically needed task-specific fine-tuning datasets of thousands or tens of thousands of examples [3]. That shift in how systems are used also raised new questions about reliability.

How language systems are measured

Measuring what a language system can and cannot do is itself a research problem.

Benchmarks are the basic tool. The Natural Questions dataset, for example, collects real, anonymized queries issued to the Google search engine, each paired with a Wikipedia page and annotated for a long answer and a short answer. Its public release has 307,373 training examples [5].

But a single benchmark gives a narrow view. The Holistic Evaluation of Language Models (HELM) project measured seven things, including accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity and efficiency, across 16 core scenarios where possible, and evaluated 30 prominent models on 42 scenarios in all [4]. Its authors reported that, before their work, models had on average been evaluated on only 17.9% of the core scenarios, and that they raised this to 96.0% [4]. The same authors point out gaps they found, such as question answering for neglected English dialects and metrics for trustworthiness [4].

Reliability is another thread. A survey of hallucination in natural language generation notes that deep-learning-based generation is prone to producing unintended text, and that this degrades performance and fails to meet user expectations in many real-world settings [6]. The survey reviews work on measuring and mitigating the problem [6].

Where NLP meets other fields

NLP draws on linguistics for what language is, on statistics and machine learning for how to model it, and on computer science for the systems that run these models at scale. It also meets ethics, policy and the social sciences. A 2021 paper asked about the dangers of large language models [7]. Model cards offer a format for model reporting [8].

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has published a generative artificial intelligence (AI) profile as a companion resource to its AI Risk Management Framework, aimed at helping organisations incorporate trustworthiness into the design, development, use and evaluation of AI products [9].

For funders and research partners, the sound reading is that language technology has advanced fast on tested tasks, while methods for evaluating it fully and managing its risks are still being built.

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How can language systems be stopped from producing fluent text that is not grounded in fact?

    Surveys describe hallucination, meaning generated text that departs from the intended or true content, as a problem that degrades performance in real-world uses [6].

  • What should a complete evaluation of a language model include?

    A large evaluation effort found that, before it, models had on average been tested on under a fifth of a common set of core scenarios, so results were hard to compare [4].

  • What are the risks of ever-larger language models, and who bears them?

    The 2021 paper "On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots" examined the dangers of large language models.

Explainer

A single score is not a full evaluation.

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Attention-based neural networks

    Models built around an attention mechanism that lets each word be weighed against the others in a passage [1].

  • Pre-training and fine-tuning

    A model first learns from a large body of text, then is adapted to a specific task [2].

  • Few-shot prompting

    A model is given a handful of examples in its instructions rather than being retrained [3].

  • Benchmarks and holistic evaluation

    Shared test sets and multi-measure evaluations that allow models to be compared [5][4].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Benchmark scores measure performance on the benchmark. They do not guarantee the same results on different languages, dialects or domains [4].
  • Generated text can be fluent and wrong at the same time [6].
  • Guidance on managing the risks of generative AI is itself still being developed [9].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Transformer
A neural network design that processes text using attention rather than step-by-step recurrence.
Language model
A system that predicts likely text, trained on large amounts of writing.
Hallucination
Generated text that is unfaithful to the source or to the facts.
Benchmark
A fixed set of tasks and answers used to compare systems.
