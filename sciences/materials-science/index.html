Sciences / Field overview

Materials Science

Materials science increasingly searches for new compounds by computer before making them. Open databases of calculated properties and machine-learning models guide the search, but whether a predicted material can be made and is useful is still decided in the laboratory [1][5][6].

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 23, 2026.

An initiative to speed up discovery

Materials discovery was traditionally slow and costly. The Materials Genome Initiative (MGI) aims to reduce the cost and development time of materials discovery, optimisation and deployment, and the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) describes its own mission as tightly aligned with it [9]. Its flagship data efforts are open databases of calculated properties.

Open computed databases

The Materials Project, a core program of the initiative, uses high-throughput computing to uncover the properties of all known inorganic materials, and its dataset is open for exploration and mining [1]. The Open Quantum Materials Database (OQMD) held nearly 300,000 density functional theory (DFT) total-energy calculations when described, was released without restrictions, and was used to evaluate the accuracy of its own formation energies against experimental measurements [2]. The Joint Automated Repository for Various Integrated Simulations (JARVIS) combines DFT, classical force fields and machine learning, with around 40,000 materials and one million calculated properties in its DFT component at the time of publication [4]. AFLOW is another automatic framework for high-throughput materials discovery [3].

Learning from the data

With data come surrogate models. Reviewers describe machine-learning models that make rapid predictions from past data rather than by direct experiment or by solving fundamental equations [8], and survey machine learning in solid-state materials science from principles and descriptors to databases [7]. At the extreme, a 2023 study reported that graph networks trained at scale improved the efficiency of materials discovery by an order of magnitude, identifying 2.2 million structures below the current convex hull, with 736 already realised experimentally by others [5].

From prediction to synthesis

Predictions are cheap compared with synthesis. An autonomous laboratory, the A-Lab, used computations, literature data, machine learning and active learning to plan and interpret robotic experiments, realising 41 novel compounds from 58 targets over 17 days [6]. Its authors stressed that analysing the failed syntheses gave direct, actionable suggestions for improving screening and synthesis design [6].

What the databases contain and enable

The Materials Project says it also seeks to create open-source platforms for developing sophisticated materials analyses and to enable rapid prototyping of new materials in silico [1]. OQMD includes calculations of compounds from the Inorganic Crystal Structure Database and of decorations of commonly occurring crystal structures [2]. JARVIS bundles DFT, force-field, machine-learning and software-tool components [4]. The graph-network study states that its hundreds of millions of first-principles calculations also support learned interatomic potentials for condensed-phase molecular dynamics and zero-shot prediction of ionic conductivity [5]. In the A-Lab, targets were identified using phase-stability data from the Materials Project and Google DeepMind, and recipes were proposed by natural-language models trained on the literature [6].

How to evaluate claims

Three questions help. Was a calculation checked against experiment, as OQMD did [2]? Of the predicted candidates, how many have been made, a ratio the graph-network paper itself reports [5]? And for autonomous labs, how were failures analysed [6]? Numbers such as 2.2 million are predictions of stability, not a count of useful products.

Where materials science meets other fields

Materials science draws on physics for the underlying calculations, on chemistry for synthesis, on computer science for models and databases [7], and on energy research, since the graph-network paper highlights layered materials and solid-electrolyte candidates [5].

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How many predicted materials can actually be made?

    A deep-learning study reported 2.2 million structures below the known stability boundary, of which 736 had already been independently experimentally realised [5]. The gap between prediction and synthesis is the open question.

  • Can autonomous laboratories close the loop between prediction and synthesis?

    A robotic platform realised 41 novel compounds from 58 targets in 17 days of operation, and its analysis of failed syntheses gave suggestions for improving screening and synthesis design [6].

  • How accurate are the calculations behind the databases?

    The Open Quantum Materials Database used comparison with experiment to evaluate the accuracy of its density functional theory formation energies [2].

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • High-throughput computing

    Running many density functional theory calculations automatically to build databases of properties [1][2][3].

  • Machine-learned surrogates

    Models that predict properties from past data instead of solving the fundamental equations each time [8].

  • Large-scale graph networks

    Models trained at scale that improve the efficiency of finding stable crystals [5].

  • Autonomous synthesis

    Robotics, literature-trained language models and active learning used to plan and run experiments [6].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Computed stability is not the same as a material that has been made [5].
  • Autonomous synthesis succeeded for 41 of 58 targets, and failures were informative [6].
  • Database values from density functional theory must be compared with experiment [2].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Density functional theory
A quantum-mechanical method for calculating the properties of materials.
High-throughput
Automated and repeated at large scale.
Convex hull
In this context, the boundary of energetic stability against competing compounds.
Surrogate model
A fast learned model standing in for a slow calculation.
