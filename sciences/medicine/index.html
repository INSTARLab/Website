Sciences / Field overview

Medicine

Medicine is the science and practice of diagnosing, treating and preventing disease. Artificial intelligence is entering clinical settings, and the central question has become how such tools are evaluated and regulated before and after they are used on patients [2][4].

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 23, 2026.

Clinical AI in context

Deep learning entered medicine most visibly through image interpretation. A 2016 study trained a deep convolutional neural network on 128,175 retinal images, graded by a panel of ophthalmologists and senior residents, to detect diabetic retinopathy and diabetic macular edema [1]. A review in Nature Medicine described the impact of AI as beginning at three levels: for clinicians, mainly through rapid image interpretation; for health systems, through workflow; and for patients, through processing their own data. The same review listed bias, privacy and security, and lack of transparency among current limitations [2].

This page describes how the field is built and measured. It does not offer medical advice, and nothing here should be read as guidance about any person's health.

How regulators describe the landscape

In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) publishes a list of AI-enabled medical devices authorised for marketing. The agency states that the list is meant to give transparency to providers and patients and is not comprehensive: devices were identified mainly through AI-related terms in their authorisation summaries [4]. The FDA also says it will explore ways to identify devices that incorporate foundation models, including large language models [4].

Guidance addresses the full life of such devices. A draft guidance on AI-enabled device software functions makes recommendations on marketing submission contents and on managing risk across the total product life cycle [6]. A final guidance recommends that a predetermined change control plan describe planned modifications, the methodology to develop, validate and implement them, and an assessment of their impact, so that approved changes need not each trigger a new submission [5]. Internationally, the International Medical Device Regulators Forum released a final document in January 2025 setting out 10 guiding principles for Good Machine Learning Practice, building on principles issued in October 2021 by the FDA, Health Canada and the United Kingdom's regulator [7].

Measurement and evaluation

Performance on a development dataset is only the first step. The Epic Sepsis Model, a proprietary prediction tool implemented at hundreds of United States hospitals, was externally validated in a cohort of 27,697 patients with 38,455 hospitalisations at one academic health system. The authors reported a hospitalisation-level area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.63 and low sensitivity compared with usual practice [8]. Bias can also enter through the choice of what a model predicts: a commercial algorithm that used cost as a proxy for health need produced risk scores at which Black patients were considerably sicker than White patients, and the authors estimated that correcting it would raise the share of Black patients receiving extra help from 17.7% to 46.5% [9].

Reporting standards help readers judge studies. CONSORT-AI is an extension of the CONSORT 2010 statement for trials of AI interventions, developed through a staged consensus process, alongside the SPIRIT-AI extension for trial protocols [10].

Language models raise new measurement problems. One research team built a benchmark combining six existing medical question datasets with a new set of online health questions and proposed human evaluation along axes including factuality, harm and bias [3].

Where medicine meets other fields

Clinical AI draws on statistics, software engineering, regulatory science and ethics. Its reliance on large health datasets connects it to Genetics and to data governance, and its dependence on trustworthy software connects it to the study of how systems are verified.

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How should an AI tool be evaluated at a hospital other than the one where it was developed?

    An external validation of a widely implemented proprietary sepsis prediction model at one academic health system was published because the model's ability to identify sepsis had not been adequately evaluated despite widespread use [8].

  • How can an AI device be updated safely after it is authorised?

    FDA guidance recommends that a predetermined change control plan describe planned modifications, the methods to develop, validate and implement them, and an assessment of their impact [5].

  • How can hidden bias in clinical algorithms be detected?

    A widely used algorithm was shown to exhibit racial bias because it predicted health care costs rather than illness, so Black patients were considerably sicker than White patients at the same risk score [9].

  • How should language models be assessed for clinical knowledge?

    Researchers proposed a benchmark and a human evaluation framework covering factuality, comprehension, reasoning, possible harm and bias, because automated benchmarks alone were limited [3].

Explainer

How a sepsis alert fared, and where an eye-screening line sits.

Sepsis is a life-threatening reaction to an infection. A widely used hospital tool, the Epic Sepsis Model, gives each hospital stay a risk score every 15 minutes. A 2021 study tested it on 38,455 hospitalisations of 27,697 adult patients at Michigan Medicine, between December 6, 2018, and October 20, 2019[8]. These are results for one tool at one health system, not advice about any person's health.

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Deep learning on medical images

    Neural networks trained on labelled images, such as retinal photographs graded by ophthalmologists [1].

  • External validation

    Testing a model on patients and settings different from those used to build it [8].

  • Prospective trials with AI reporting standards

    Clinical trials of AI interventions reported under the CONSORT-AI extension [10].

  • Regulatory lifecycle management

    Documentation and planning for an AI-enabled device across its total product life cycle [6][7].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Current limitations of medical AI include bias, privacy and security concerns, and lack of transparency [2].
  • The FDA's list of AI-enabled devices is not comprehensive and was compiled mainly from AI-related terms in marketing summaries [4].
  • Results on retrospective datasets do not by themselves show impact on health outcomes; AI interventions need rigorous, prospective evaluation [10].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

AI-enabled device
A medical device that includes software functions using artificial intelligence.
External validation
Testing a prediction model on data from a different place or time than the data used to build it.
Predetermined change control plan
A plan, reviewed with the original submission, describing changes a manufacturer intends to make to an AI device later.
Total product life cycle
The whole span of a device's existence, from design through use and updates.
