Agriculture

Agriculture is now measured from orbit as well as from the field. Satellite imagery, public statistics and machine-learning models are used to estimate crop yields and spot problems early, and the quality of those estimates depends on the ground data behind them [1][2].

Measuring farms from far away

Agriculture is a data problem as much as a biological one. Governments and researchers need to know how much of each crop is growing, how well, and where trouble is developing, long before harvest. Two kinds of evidence are involved. One is official statistics: the United States Department of Agriculture's National Agricultural Statistics Service, for example, publishes a searchable database of agricultural data called Quick Stats [9]. The other is Earth observation. A meta-review of remote sensing for agricultural applications gathers this fast-growing literature [5], and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) Harvest consortium describes its aim as using Earth observation data and public-private partnerships to support agricultural resilience and decision-making [8].

Turning imagery into yield estimates

A useful test case is smallholder farming, where fields are tiny and records are scarce. Researchers used one-metre satellite imagery together with intensive field sampling on thousands of fields in western Kenya over two years. They found that agreement between satellite and survey-based yield estimates depended significantly on the quality of the field measurements, and that satellite-based measures could detect yield responses to fertiliser and hybrid seed that were statistically indistinguishable from survey-based estimates [1]. That is an encouraging result, but it is a single region and a single crop, and it shows how much the ground data matter.

Machine learning and its comparisons

Machine learning is widely used to predict yield from weather, soil and other inputs. A systematic literature review retrieved 567 relevant studies, analysed 50 in detail, and found that temperature, rainfall and soil type were the most used features and artificial neural networks the most applied algorithm; an additional search of deep-learning studies found convolutional neural networks the most widely used [2]. A broader review sorts agricultural machine-learning work into crop, livestock, water and soil management [7]. In one direct comparison, random forests were tested against multiple linear regression for wheat, maize and potato at global and regional scales [3]. For disease detection, a deep neural network trained on part of a public set of 54,306 leaf images, photographed under controlled conditions, identified 14 crop species and 26 diseases with 99.35% accuracy on the held-out rest of the set. On two small sets of photos gathered online, taken in different conditions, its accuracy fell to about 31% [6].

From research to decisions

The same tools feed practical monitoring. NASA Harvest says it produces timely agricultural information on crop health and production, and that satellite data help identify vulnerabilities and inform adaptive strategies in farm risk assessments [8]. On the research side, a review of machine learning in agriculture notes that the method has emerged alongside big data technologies and high-performance computing, opening new opportunities for data-intensive science in the agri-technologies domain [7]. The practical value of these tools therefore depends on both the models and the public and field data behind them.

Reading the evidence carefully

Accuracy figures only mean something relative to how they were measured. The Kenya study shows that the benchmark itself can be noisy [1]. The yield-prediction review shows that studies differ so much in design that headline numbers cannot be lined up side by side [2]. And the leaf-image result comes from images collected under controlled conditions, which its authors presented as demonstrating feasibility [6].

Where agriculture meets other fields

Climate is the closest neighbour. Analysis of weather-linked yield models suggested that, between 1980 and 2008, climate trends reduced global maize and wheat production by 3.8 and 5.5 percent respectively relative to a world without those trends, while for soybeans and rice gains and losses largely balanced out [4]. Agriculture also depends on computer science and statistics for modelling, and on open data practices so results can be checked.

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How well can satellites estimate yields on small farms?

    One study of smallholder maize in western Kenya found that agreement between satellite-based and survey-based yields depended heavily on the quality of the field measurements [1]. Whether this holds across crops and regions is still being tested.

  • Which machine-learning methods predict crop yield reliably across regions?

    A systematic review found a wide spread of algorithms and input features in use, so results are hard to compare directly [2]. Random forests have been compared against simple linear regression at global and regional scales [3].

  • How much has recent climate change already shifted yields?

    Models linking yields to weather suggested global maize and wheat production was lower than it would otherwise have been, but the estimates depend on model assumptions [4].

Explainer

How well a program names a leaf disease, and where that stops.

The 2016 study trained neural networks to name the crop and the disease from a photo of one leaf. It used PlantVillage, a public set of 54,306 photos of 14 crops and 26 diseases, taken under controlled conditions. Each crop-and-disease pair, or healthy crop, is one of 38 classes[6]. The top score, 99.35%, is on photos held back from the same set. The second panel shows what happened on photos from elsewhere.

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Satellite remote sensing

    Using imagery of fields to infer crop type, condition and yield [5][1].

  • Yield-prediction models

    Statistical and machine-learning models that link weather, soil and other features to yield [2][3].

  • Image-based diagnosis

    Deep neural networks trained on leaf photographs to identify crop diseases [6].

  • Official statistics

    Government databases of agricultural data, such as the Quick Stats database run by the National Agricultural Statistics Service [9].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Satellite yield estimates are only as good as the field data used to check them [1].
  • Published yield-prediction studies use many different algorithms and features, which limits direct comparison [2].
  • The disease-detection result is for a held-out test set from a public dataset of images collected under controlled conditions [6].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Remote sensing
Gathering information about land or crops from a distance, usually by satellite or aircraft.
Yield
The amount of crop harvested per unit of land.
Random forest
A machine-learning method that combines many decision trees to make a prediction.
Smallholder
A farmer working a small plot, often with limited access to inputs and records.
