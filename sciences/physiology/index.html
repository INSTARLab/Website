Physiology

Physiology studies how the body's organs and systems work, often by recording signals such as the heart's electrical activity over time. Open signal archives, wearable sensors and machine learning have turned that recording into a data problem [1][7][3].

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 23, 2026.

What computational physiology works with

Physiological data are mostly signals: heartbeats, breathing, muscle and brain activity recorded over time. In 2000 the Research Resource for Complex Physiologic Signals, supported by the National Institutes of Health, introduced PhysioBank, an archive of well-characterised digital recordings of physiological signals, and PhysioToolkit, a library of software for analysing them. The archive included recordings from healthy subjects and from patients with conditions such as life-threatening arrhythmias, congestive heart failure, sleep apnea, neurological disorders and ageing [1].

Hospital records are a second source. MIMIC-IV is a publicly available database drawn from the electronic health record of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. It includes patient measurements, orders, diagnoses, procedures, treatments and de-identified free-text notes, and it is intended to lower barriers to clinical research [2].

Signals and the metrics built from them

Heart rate variability (HRV) shows how such signals are turned into measures. HRV consists of changes in the intervals between consecutive heartbeats. A review describes a healthy heart as not a metronome: its oscillations are complex and constantly changing, which lets the cardiovascular system adjust rapidly to challenges. The review surveys widely used time-domain, frequency-domain and non-linear metrics and distinguishes 24-hour, short-term and ultra-short-term recordings [3]. Software helps keep such calculations consistent: NeuroKit2 is an open-source Python package with validated pipelines for ECG, photoplethysmography, electrodermal activity, muscle activity and respiration, designed to improve transparency and reproducibility [8].

Machine learning and wearables

Two developments have drawn the most attention. The first is deep-learning analysis of heart recordings. A neural network trained on 91,232 single-lead ECGs from 53,549 patients classified 12 rhythm classes. On a test set annotated by a committee of cardiologists, its average F1 score was 0.837 against 0.780 for the average cardiologist, and the authors say that confirmation in clinical settings is needed [4].

The second is consumer wearables. A review of the promise and barriers of consumer health wearables set out the opportunities and obstacles of devices that individuals buy and use themselves [7]. In one large study, 419,297 participants used a smartwatch app, and 0.52% received irregular-pulse notifications. Atrial fibrillation was found in 34% of the 450 notified participants whose ECG patch data could be analysed, and the study reported no serious app-related adverse events [5].

Measurement and evaluation

Accuracy is a research question in its own right. A study of optical heart-rate sensors tested consumer and research-grade wearables under several circumstances. It found no statistically significant difference in accuracy across skin tones but did find significant differences between devices and between activity types, with absolute error during activity 30% higher on average than during rest [6]. The authors conclude that devices are reasonably accurate at rest and at prolonged elevated heart rate, and that differences between devices matter for researchers, clinicians and consumers drawing conclusions from them [6].

Taken together, these studies show a pattern: a signal that is easy to collect is not automatically easy to interpret, and each claim about accuracy belongs to a specific device, activity and population. Nothing here is medical advice, and the findings describe studies, not recommendations for individuals.

Where physiology meets other fields

Physiology draws on signal processing, statistics and machine learning, and its clinical relevance connects it to Medicine. Open archives such as PhysioBank and MIMIC-IV [1][2] tie it to the data-sharing questions discussed on the Open Data page. The study of wearables also links it to Kinesiology, where sensors measure movement and activity.

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How accurate are consumer wearables across devices, activities and people?

    A study of heart-rate sensors based on photoplethysmography (an optical method) found no statistically significant difference in accuracy across skin tones, but significant differences between devices and activity types, with error during activity about 30% higher than at rest [6].

  • When a wearable flags an irregular rhythm, how often does a clinician-grade recording confirm it?

    In a study of 419,297 participants, 2,161 received irregular-pulse notifications, and among 450 who returned analysable ECG patches, atrial fibrillation was present in 34% [5].

  • Can deep-learning rhythm classification hold up in real clinical settings?

    A neural network classifying 12 rhythm classes outperformed the average cardiologist on a test set, and its authors state the approach needs confirmation in clinical settings [4].

  • How should signal-processing pipelines be standardised so results are comparable?

    NeuroKit2 is an open-source package with validated pipelines for signals such as ECG, photoplethysmography and respiration, aimed at improving transparency and reproducibility [8].

Explainer

How far off a wearable heart-rate reading can be.

Devices like these shine a light into the skin and read the pulse from it. A 2020 study checked six of them against an ECG, which the paper treats as the true heart rate, in 53 volunteers[6]. These are results for those devices on those people, not advice about any device you own.

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Open physiological signal archives

    Curated recordings of cardiopulmonary, neural and other signals, paired with analysis software, shared for research [1].

  • Heart rate variability analysis

    Time-domain, frequency-domain and non-linear metrics computed from intervals between consecutive heartbeats [3].

  • Deep learning on ECG waveforms

    End-to-end neural networks trained on large numbers of annotated ECG recordings [4].

  • Clinical database mining

    Research on de-identified hospital records including measurements, orders and diagnoses [2].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Wearable accuracy varies by device and by activity, with larger average error during activity than at rest [6].
  • Most notified participants in a large smartwatch study did not return analysable ECG data, so the confirmed rate rests on a subset [5].
  • Deep-learning ECG results come from specific test datasets and need confirmation in clinical practice [4].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Photoplethysmography (PPG)
An optical method that senses changes in blood volume under the skin, used for heart rate in many wearables.
Electrocardiogram (ECG)
A recording of the heart's electrical activity.
Heart rate variability
The variation in time between consecutive heartbeats.
Atrial fibrillation
An irregular heart rhythm originating in the upper chambers of the heart.
