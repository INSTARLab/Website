Sciences / Field overview

Chemistry

Computational chemistry uses quantum-mechanical calculations to predict how molecules behave, and machine learning is now used to speed those calculations up. The field's central challenge is keeping that speed without losing trustworthy accuracy [2][5].

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 23, 2026.

Computation as a laboratory

Much of chemistry now begins on a computer. The accurate description of chemical processes often requires methods such as density functional theory (DFT), which are computationally demanding and can make long simulations of large systems unfeasible [1]. That cost is the bottleneck that data-driven methods target. In 2007 a neural-network representation of DFT potential-energy surfaces was introduced that gave energy and forces as a function of all atomic positions and was several orders of magnitude faster than DFT, demonstrated for bulk silicon [1].

Learning the potential

The idea matured into a field. A decade later the ANI-1 work trained a deep neural network on quantum-mechanical DFT calculations to learn an accurate and transferable potential for organic molecules [3]. A review of machine-learned force fields describes the key idea as learning the statistical relation between chemical structure and potential energy without a preconceived notion of fixed chemical bonds, so that the approximation is in principle limited only by the reference data, and it closes with the challenges that remain for the next generation [2]. A general review of machine learning for molecular and materials science places these developments in a wider context [9].

Data and benchmarks

Learning needs data. The QM9 dataset reports computed geometric, energetic, electronic and thermodynamic properties for 134,000 stable small organic molecules: the 133,885 species with up to nine heavy atoms (carbon, oxygen, nitrogen and fluorine) out of the GDB-17 chemical universe of 166 billion organic molecules, all calculated at the B3LYP/6-31G(2df,p) level of quantum chemistry. For the 6,095 isomers of its most common formula, C7H10O2, it also reports energies at the more accurate G4MP2 level [4]. For methods themselves, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) maintains the Computational Chemistry Comparison and Benchmark Database, a standard reference database whose current release was issued in May 2022 [10]. Its role is to let calculated results be compared with experimental data.

Design and synthesis

Machine learning is also applied to deciding which molecules to make and how. One approach converts discrete molecular representations to and from a continuous multidimensional space, so that a trained encoder, decoder and property predictor can generate and optimise new molecules [6]. For reactions, a framework combining reaction templates with neural networks was proposed for anticipating reaction outcomes [7], and a Nature paper title describes planning chemical syntheses with deep neural networks and symbolic artificial intelligence [8].

Testing and constructing models

Methods papers increasingly serve as guides. The review of machine-learned force fields includes a step-by-step guide for constructing and testing such models from scratch [2]. The 2007 neural-network method was described as general and applicable to periodic and nonperiodic systems [1]. QM9 also documents a case for studying isomers: for the predominant stoichiometry, C7H10O2, it lists 6,095 constitutional isomers [4]. Finally, the molecular-design method includes a predictor that estimates chemical properties from the continuous representation, so that candidates can be steered toward desired values [6].

What counts as evidence

Chemistry has an unusually clear referee: experiment. The reaction-prediction authors made this point directly, writing that proposed steps often fail when attempted in the laboratory despite seeming viable [7]. For learned potentials, evidence comes from comparison with the quantum calculations they approximate [1][3]. A review aimed at researchers working across both fields offers tutorials on computational chemistry and machine learning together with a critical look at applications [5].

Where chemistry meets other fields

Chemistry connects to computer science through the models and to materials science and physics through shared simulation methods [9][5]. Learned potentials are one example of a tool used across all three [2].

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • Can learned force fields match quantum accuracy outside their training data?

    A review of machine-learned force fields says such approximations are in principle limited only by the quality and quantity of their reference data, and ends by discussing challenges that remain [2].

  • How reliably can models predict the outcome of reactions that actually work in the laboratory?

    Work on reaction prediction stressed that the true measure of success for a synthesis program is whether the predicted outcome matches what is observed experimentally [7].

  • What does it take to combine chemistry and machine learning well?

    A review of the intersection says progress requires a confluence of expertise in computer science and the physical sciences [5].

Explainer

Teaching a model quantum chemistry.

Quantum-chemistry methods such as density functional theory (DFT) give accurate energies for a molecule but are slow. Machine-learning models are trained on many such calculations so that they can give similar answers far faster. Two questions follow: how much of chemistry the training data covers, and how close the trained model gets.

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Density functional theory (DFT)

    A computationally demanding quantum method for energies and forces; long simulations of large systems can be unfeasible with it [1].

  • Neural-network potentials

    Models trained on DFT data that compute energies several orders of magnitude faster than DFT [1][3].

  • Generative molecular design

    Encoding molecules as continuous vectors so new candidates can be generated and optimised [6].

  • Benchmark databases

    Curated comparisons of calculated and experimental data used to check methods [10][4].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Learned potentials depend on the quality and quantity of their reference data [2].
  • A prediction that looks plausible can still fail when attempted in the laboratory [7].
  • Datasets such as QM9 cover small organic molecules, mostly at a single level of theory; only 6,095 isomers also have higher-level G4MP2 energies [4].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Potential-energy surface
The energy of a molecule or material as a function of where its atoms are.
Force field
A simplified model that estimates forces between atoms cheaply.
Density functional theory
A quantum-mechanical method for calculating the electronic structure of molecules and solids.
Benchmark
A reference dataset used to compare methods fairly.
