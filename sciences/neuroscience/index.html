Sciences / Field overview

Neuroscience

Neuroscience studies the nervous system, from single neurons to whole-brain activity and behaviour. It now produces very large open datasets, which makes data standards, analysis choices and statistical power central scientific questions [3][4][5].

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 23, 2026.

The state of the field

Neuroscience has moved toward large shared resources. The Human Connectome Project set out to map macroscopic human brain circuits and their relationship to behaviour in a large population of healthy adults, with imaging and behavioural data released through a database called ConnectomeDB. The project's overview paper describes a commitment to making its datasets freely accessible [1]. OpenNeuro, a BRAIN Initiative data archive, shares brain imaging data following the FAIR principles (findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable) and held more than 600 datasets from more than 20,000 participants at the time of its description, with more than 150 publications reusing them [3].

Sharing depends on common formats. The authors of the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) noted that, without a widely adopted standard for organising MRI data, sharing and reuse between labs was difficult and automatic pipelines were complicated [2]. OpenNeuro's authors highlight the importance of BIDS for curation, sharing and reuse [3].

Recent turning points

Two kinds of open resource stand out. The first is large-scale recording and wiring data from animals. The Allen Brain Observatory released an open dataset of spiking from tens of thousands of units across eight mouse brain regions, in which inter-area functional connectivity mirrored the anatomical hierarchy [6]. A whole-brain wiring diagram of an adult fruit fly covers 139,255 neurons and about 50 million chemical synapses, with data open for download and programmatic access [7]. The MICrONS dataset co-registers calcium imaging from about 75,000 neurons in mouse visual cortex with an electron-microscopy reconstruction of more than 200,000 cells and 0.5 billion synapses [8].

The second is the use of artificial neural networks as models of the brain. A Nature Neuroscience perspective reviewed progress in using goal-driven hierarchical convolutional networks to model single-unit and population responses in higher visual cortex [9]. A related article argued that systems neuroscience would benefit from focusing on the objective functions, learning rules and architectures that define such networks [10].

Measurement and evaluation

The most useful recent work in this area is about how much to trust results. A study using three of the largest neuroimaging datasets, around 50,000 people in total, addressed the problem that typical studies of brain-behaviour associations are small; the article's title states that reproducible brain-wide association studies require thousands of individuals [4]. A separate study asked 70 teams to analyse one fMRI dataset to test nine hypotheses. No two teams used the same workflow, and the results varied sizeably, although a meta-analysis across teams showed significant consensus in activated regions [5].

These findings are not arguments against neuroimaging. They are arguments for larger samples, open data and standard formats, which the open resources above make easier, and for transparency about analysis choices [2][3][4][5].

Where neuroscience meets other fields

Neuroscience is tightly bound to computer science and statistics. Data volumes need standards and archives [2][3]. Models from machine learning provide candidate explanations for neural responses [9][10]. Connectomics depends on reconstructing neurons from microscopy images and on proofreading those reconstructions, as the MICrONS authors describe [8]. Links to psychology are discussed on the Cognitive Sciences page, where computational models are tested against behaviour and brain data.

Readers new to this literature may find it useful to notice how often the same themes recur: data standards, sample size, transparency about analysis choices, and humility about what a model of a small system says about a whole brain [2][4][5][8]. The sources collected here are a starting point for further reading, not a complete survey.

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How large must brain-imaging studies be to link brain differences to behaviour reliably?

    An analysis of three of the largest neuroimaging datasets, with about 50,000 participants in total, addressed the fact that the median neuroimaging study includes about 25 people, which may be too few for reproducible brain-behaviour associations [4].

  • How much do analysis choices change what a neuroimaging dataset appears to show?

    When 70 independent teams analysed the same dataset to test the same 9 hypotheses, no two chose identical workflows and results varied sizeably [5].

  • Can brain-scale wiring diagrams be linked to function?

    A mouse dataset pairs calcium imaging of about 75,000 neurons with an electron-microscopy reconstruction of more than 200,000 cells [8], and a fruit-fly wiring diagram covers 139,255 neurons [7]. Relating structure to function across whole brains is still being worked out.

  • Are deep neural networks useful models of the brain?

    Goal-driven convolutional networks have been used to model neural responses in higher visual areas [9], and an optimisation framework has been proposed for systems neuroscience [10].

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and functional MRI

    Non-invasive measurement of brain structure and activity, used in large datasets such as those of the Human Connectome Project [1][2].

  • Standardised data organisation

    The Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) is a standard for organising and describing MRI datasets so that they can be shared and reused [2].

  • Large-scale electrophysiology

    Recording spiking from tens of thousands of units across brain regions in mice viewing visual stimuli [6].

  • Connectomics

    Mapping connections between neurons from electron microscopy images [7][8].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Small sample sizes can produce brain-behaviour associations that do not replicate [4].
  • Flexible analysis pipelines can lead different teams to different conclusions from the same data [5].
  • The wiring diagrams described here cover a fruit-fly brain and part of mouse visual cortex, not human brains [7][8].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Connectome
A map of the connections between neurons in a brain or brain region.
fMRI
Functional magnetic resonance imaging, which tracks changes linked to brain activity.
BIDS
Brain Imaging Data Structure, a standard way of naming and arranging neuroimaging files.
Brain-wide association study
A study linking differences in brain structure or function across people to differences in cognition or mental health.
