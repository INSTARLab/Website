Sciences / Field overview

Genetics

Genetics studies how differences in DNA relate to traits and disease. The field now depends on very large shared datasets and on computational methods to interpret them, which raises questions about accuracy, fairness and privacy [10][9].

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 23, 2026.

The state of the field

Genetics has become a data science. The National Human Genome Research Institute describes genomic data science as a field that uses computational and statistical methods to decode the functional information in DNA sequence, and cites estimates that genomics research will generate between 2 and 40 exabytes of data within a decade [10]. The same fact sheet notes that each person's sequence data raises issues of privacy and identity, so analysis carries ethical responsibilities [10].

The reference itself has changed. Until recently the human reference genome left important heterochromatic regions unfinished. The Telomere-to-Telomere (T2T) Consortium reported a complete 3.055 billion base-pair sequence, T2T-CHM13, with gapless assemblies for all chromosomes except Y and nearly 200 million base pairs of new sequence [1].

Biobanks and shared catalogues

Large cohorts now supply the data. The UK Biobank holds deep genetic and phenotypic data on approximately 500,000 people aged 40 to 69 at recruitment, with follow-up through linked health records [2]. Genome-wide genotype data were collected on all participants, and the authors describe the open resource as unique in its size and scope [2]. The Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD) combined 125,748 exomes and 15,708 genomes, identified 443,769 high-confidence predicted loss-of-function variants and used them to quantify how constrained genes are [3]. The authors stress that predicted loss-of-function variants are enriched for annotation errors and extremely low in frequency, so analysing them requires careful annotation and very large samples [3]. In plain terms, a catalogue is only as reliable as the filtering applied to it.

The All of Us Research Program released 245,388 clinical-grade genome sequences. Its authors report that 77% of participants come from communities historically under-represented in biomedical research, and that the data revealed more than 275 million previously unreported variants [4].

Interpreting variants

Sequencing a person is easier than explaining what the result means. ClinVar, maintained at the National Institutes of Health, is a public archive of human variants and the interpretations that laboratories and expert groups submit about their significance [5]. Professional standards also matter: a joint recommendation from the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics, the Association for Molecular Pathology and the College of American Pathologists was written because the shift to next-generation sequencing brought new challenges in sequence interpretation [6].

Machine learning has been added to this toolkit. AlphaMissense, an adaptation of AlphaFold, predicts the pathogenicity of missense variants and is released with predictions for all possible human single amino acid substitutions [7]. Enformer predicts gene expression from DNA sequence while integrating interactions up to 100 kb away [8]. These are research tools, and the papers describe benchmark results, not clinical validation.

Fairness and evaluation

Evaluation in genetics is increasingly about who is represented. An analysis of polygenic risk scores found that available scores were several times more accurate in people of European ancestry than in other ancestries, and attributed this to Eurocentric bias in genome-wide association studies; early efforts to diversify were described as promising [9]. The composition of cohorts such as All of Us is therefore part of the scientific record, not a side issue [4].

Where genetics meets other fields

Genetics connects to biology through protein structure, since AlphaMissense builds on a structure-prediction model [7]. It connects to computer science through the scale of the data [10] and to medicine through variant interpretation standards [6]. Because genomic data are tied to identity, it also connects to ethics and data governance [10].

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How can the meaning of rare genetic variants be established when most are of unknown significance?

    The authors of AlphaMissense state that the vast majority of missense variants seen in the human genome are of unknown clinical significance, and report classifying 89% of them as likely benign or likely pathogenic [7]. Whether such classifications are trustworthy enough for clinical use is a separate question.

  • How can genetic prediction be made equally useful across ancestries?

    Polygenic risk scores available in 2019 were several times more accurate in people of European ancestry than in other groups, a result of bias in the underlying studies [9].

  • How far can gene expression be predicted from DNA sequence alone?

    A deep-learning model that integrates interactions up to 100 kb away improved expression prediction from sequence, though the authors describe how noncoding DNA determines expression as a major unsolved problem [8].

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Genome assembly and reference building

    Constructing complete, gapless sequences of human chromosomes, as in the telomere-to-telomere assembly [1].

  • Population-scale variant catalogues

    Aggregating exomes and genomes from many people to estimate how tolerant each gene is of disruption [3].

  • Standardised variant classification

    Professional guidelines that set out how laboratories should interpret sequence variants [6].

  • Deep-learning variant and expression prediction

    Models trained on sequence and population data to score variants or predict gene expression [7][8].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Predicted loss-of-function variants are enriched for annotation errors, so careful filtering is needed [3].
  • Polygenic scores perform unevenly across ancestries because of biases in the studies that produced them [9].
  • Sequence data are tied to privacy and identity, which brings ethical responsibilities for anyone analysing them [10].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Exome
The protein-coding portion of the genome.
Missense variant
A DNA change that swaps one amino acid in a protein for another.
Polygenic risk score
A number that combines the small effects of many genetic variants into one estimate of inherited tendency toward a trait.
Biobank
A large collection of biological samples and linked data from volunteers, kept for research.
