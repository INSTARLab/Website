Sciences / Field overview

Kinesiology

Kinesiology is the study of human movement and physical activity, covering how people move, what loads their bodies bear and how much they are active. Video, wearable sensors and simulation now let researchers measure and model movement outside a specialised laboratory [1][6][7].

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 23, 2026.

How movement is measured

Kinesiology depends on measuring movement accurately. A narrative review in Sports Medicine - Open traces how methods for extracting kinematic information from images have progressed from laborious manual approaches to marker-based optoelectronic systems. It notes that the systems most widely used in sports biomechanics and rehabilitation cannot collect data automatically without markers, controlled conditions or extensive processing times, and it describes developing an accurate, unobtrusive, externally valid system as an open challenge [1].

Physical activity is measured differently, at population scale. A report using accelerometers in the 2003-2004 United States National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) described objectively measured activity in 6,329 participants who provided at least one day of data. Males were more active than females, and activity declined across age groups from childhood through adolescence and with age [8].

Recent turning points

Computer vision has changed what can be done with ordinary video. OpenPose introduced a real-time method that detects the 2D pose of multiple people in an image using Part Affinity Fields [3]. DeepLabCut brought markerless pose estimation based on transfer learning with deep neural networks, which its authors report gives excellent results with minimal training data and across multiple species [2].

Simulation has changed what can be inferred from movement. OpenSim, introduced in 2007, is freely available, open-source software for building models of musculoskeletal structures and creating dynamic simulations of movement [4]. A later overview explains that it can calculate variables that are hard to measure experimentally, such as muscle forces and tendon stretch and recoil, and can predict novel movements from models of motor control [5]. OpenCap joins the two threads: it processes smartphone videos in the cloud, using pose estimation, deep learning and biomechanical models to estimate three-dimensional kinematics, and physics-based simulation to estimate muscle activations and dynamics [6].

Measurement and evaluation

Evaluation questions in kinesiology are often about whether different studies are comparable. A systematic review of the ActiGraph GT3X+ accelerometer, among the most frequently used research devices, found that data collection and processing criteria had evolved in many ways with no consensus, and aimed to compile and classify them to improve comparability [7]. In sport, a systematic review screened 2,040 papers and selected 286 studies and 23 reviews on the use of wearable accelerometers, gyroscopes and magnetometers to evaluate sport performance [10].

Recommendations about activity rest on a different kind of evidence. The World Health Organization's 2020 guidelines were developed by an expert group that reviewed evidence on physical activity and sedentary behaviour for specified health outcomes and population groups. For adults they recommend 150 to 300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination, each week [9]. This page summarises published guidance and is not exercise or medical advice.

Where kinesiology meets other fields

Kinesiology meets computer science through pose estimation and simulation [2][3][4], and statistics through the handling of sensor data [7]. It meets neuroscience: DeepLabCut was introduced for quantifying behaviour in neuroscience, and OpenSim's authors describe uniting methods from biology, neuroscience, mechanics and robotics [2][5]. Its links to Physiology run through wearable sensors, which both fields use to measure the body in everyday settings.

Open tools make comparison easier. Several of the systems above are described as open source or freely available [4][5][6], which allows others to inspect and reuse the models.

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • Can accurate movement measurement be made routine outside the laboratory?

    A review describes developing a motion analysis system that is accurate, timely, unobtrusive and externally valid as an open challenge, because widely used systems need markers, controlled conditions or long processing times [1].

  • Can forces inside the body be estimated from ordinary video?

    OpenCap is an open-source platform that estimates kinematics and dynamics from videos captured by two or more smartphones, combining pose estimation, deep learning, biomechanical models and physics-based simulation [6].

  • How can physical-activity data from accelerometers be made comparable across studies?

    A systematic review of one widely used accelerometer found that data collection and processing criteria had evolved in many ways, with no consensus [7].

Explainer

How close a smartphone gets to a movement lab.

A movement laboratory tracks reflective markers with eight or more cameras and measures forces with plates in the floor. OpenCap instead uses video from two or more smartphones, a pose-estimation program and a body model to estimate the same things. The authors tested it against the laboratory on 10 healthy adults using 2 iPhones[6].

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Markerless pose estimation

    Neural networks that locate body parts in video without reflective markers [2][3].

  • Musculoskeletal simulation

    Open-source software for building models of bones, joints and muscles and simulating movement dynamics [4][5].

  • Accelerometer-based activity measurement

    Objective measurement of activity in population surveys [8] and in sport with inertial sensors [10].

  • Evidence-based guidelines

    Systematic review of evidence to set recommendations on physical activity and sedentary behaviour [9].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Widely used motion analysis systems often require markers, controlled conditions or extensive processing [1].
  • Accelerometer processing choices differ between studies and there is no consensus, which limits comparability [7].
  • Models such as OpenSim calculate quantities that are difficult to measure directly, so their outputs depend on modelling assumptions [5].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Kinematics
The description of motion itself, such as joint angles and speeds, without reference to the forces involved.
Dynamics
The forces and torques that produce movement, such as those generated by muscles.
Pose estimation
Computer vision methods that locate a person's body parts in images or video.
Accelerometer
A sensor that measures acceleration, used in wearables to infer movement and activity.
