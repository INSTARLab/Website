Sciences / Field overview

Physics

Physics runs on computation: simulations, large detector datasets and, increasingly, machine learning. How well those tools can be trusted and combined with theory is a live question across the field [1][10].

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 23, 2026.

Computation at the centre

Physics has long used computers, but the balance has shifted toward large simulations, enormous detector datasets and machine learning. A review in Reviews of Modern Physics summarises an October 2018 workshop on machine learning held by the American Physical Society (APS), and describes the field as very broad and emerging [1]. Within particle physics, a community white paper notes that machine learning began with high-level analysis in the 1990s and 2000s and exploded in particle identification and reconstruction in the 2010s [4].

Data from the largest instruments

The scale of data is substantial. The ATLAS collaboration's search for the Standard Model Higgs boson at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) combined datasets of about 4.8 inverse femtobarns at 7 teraelectronvolts in 2011 and 5.8 inverse femtobarns at 8 teraelectronvolts in 2012, and was titled as an observation of a new particle [6]. Looking ahead, a roadmap for the High-Luminosity LHC states that the experimental programme needs investment not only in hardware but in software to acquire, manage, process and analyse the data [5].

Detection in noise

Gravitational-wave astronomy shows how analysis methods and physics meet. The first detection reported a signal observed by two Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO) detectors, with a matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio of 24 and a false alarm rate estimated at less than one event per 203,000 years [7]. Researchers later used convolutional neural networks, an approach called Deep Filtering, for rapid detection and characterisation of such signals, extending it from simulated noise to real LIGO data [8].

Learning laws and wave functions

Machine learning in physics is not only about detection. One approach represents quantum many-body states with artificial neural networks and uses a reinforcement-learning scheme to find ground states and describe time evolution for some notable cases [2]. Another automatically searched motion-tracking data from physical systems to identify analytical laws, using a proposed principle for judging which relations are non-trivial [3]. A third strand embeds equations in training: physics-informed neural networks target forward and inverse problems involving nonlinear partial differential equations [9], and a Nature Reviews Physics review covers physics-informed machine learning [10].

A closer look at one detection

The first gravitational-wave paper gives a useful sense of what a result contains. The signal swept upward in frequency from 35 to 250 hertz, with a peak strain of 1.0 times 10 to the minus 21, and matched the waveform predicted by general relativity for the inspiral and merger of two black holes [7]. The reported source masses were about 36 and 29 times the mass of the Sun, with a final black hole of about 62 solar masses [7]. The machine-learning white paper adds that a roadmap for the field must also cover software and hardware resource needs, collaboration with data science, and training for the particle-physics community [4].

Judging the evidence

In physics, claims are tested against stated significance levels, simulated backgrounds and known equations. The gravitational-wave paper quotes explicit false alarm rates [7]; the Deep Filtering authors moved from simulated to real detector data [8]. The workshop review and the white paper both present themselves as summaries and roadmaps rather than final results [1][4].

Where physics meets other fields

Physics shares simulation and learning methods with chemistry and materials science, supplies detection problems to astronomy [7], and relies on computer science for large-scale software [5].

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How should machine learning be combined with physical law?

    Physics-informed neural networks were proposed as a framework for forward and inverse problems involving nonlinear partial differential equations [9]. A later review is titled Physics-informed machine learning [10].

  • Can machine learning recover physical laws from data?

    One study searched motion-tracking data from systems ranging from simple oscillators to chaotic double pendula to find natural laws automatically [3].

  • What computing will future particle-physics experiments need?

    A community white paper laid out research and development areas for machine learning in particle physics [4], and a roadmap describes software R&D needed for the High-Luminosity Large Hadron Collider (HL-LHC) [5].

Explainer

Two detections, and how sure each was.

Physics results come with numbers that say how big a thing is and how sure the authors are that it is real. Two papers on this page show both: the first detection of gravitational waves, and the search that found a new particle at the Large Hadron Collider.

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Numerical simulation

    Computing the behaviour of physical systems from equations, at scales that need large computing resources.

  • Neural-network wave functions

    Representing quantum states with artificial neural networks [2].

  • Matched filtering and deep filtering

    Detecting signals buried in detector noise, by template matching or by neural networks [7][8].

  • Physics-informed learning

    Training neural networks while respecting governing equations [9][10].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Machine learning in physics is developing quickly, so workshop summaries date fast [1].
  • Neural-network wave functions were demonstrated for some notable cases, not all systems [2].
  • Data volumes from future experiments require software investment that is still being planned [5].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Partial differential equation
An equation describing how a quantity changes across space and time, common in physics.
Wave function
The mathematical description of a quantum system's state.
Matched filter
A detection method that compares data with expected signal shapes.
Luminosity
In collider physics, a measure of how many collisions an accelerator delivers.
