Sciences / Field overview

Economics

Economics studies how people, firms and governments make choices under scarcity, and it now does so with very large datasets. Machine learning is changing how economists predict and estimate, but it solves a different problem from the one many economic questions pose [2]. The field's open questions are about causal claims, reliability and how far new data sources can be trusted [4][6][7].

A source-backed overview of the field, not a list of INSTAR projects or results. Every source is numbered below and was last checked on October 23, 2026.

State of the field

Economics has always been quantitative, but the data have changed. Computers now take part in many economic transactions and record them, producing datasets that can be analysed in bulk [3]. Conventional regression often works well on such data, yet very large datasets raise issues of their own: they may require stronger data-handling tools, offer more potential predictors than estimation can use, and allow more flexible relationships than a simple linear model [3].

Machine learning arrived in economics with a conceptual caution attached. Mullainathan and Spiess note that machine learning revolves around prediction, whereas many economic applications revolve around estimating parameters, so applying it means finding tasks where prediction is what is needed. They also warn that because the algorithms are now easy to run, they can be applied naively or their output misread [2]. A review in the Annual Review of Economics covers supervised and unsupervised methods and matrix completion, then turns to newer methods at the boundary of machine learning and econometrics for causal inference, optimal policy estimation and consumer choice [1].

Recent turning points

Two developments shaped how the field talks about evidence. The first is the credibility revolution. Angrist and Pischke argue that the main driver of improvement in empirical microeconomics has been attention to the quality of research design, most visible where assignment is random [6]. The second is the combination of that causal tradition with machine learning [1].

Chernozhukov and colleagues addressed a specific problem: when high-dimensional nuisance quantities are estimated by machine learning and plugged naively into estimating equations, regularisation bias and overfitting produce heavy bias in the parameter of interest. They show the effect can be removed with orthogonal scores and cross-fitting [4]. Wager and Athey developed causal forests, extending random forests to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects, with results that allow valid confidence intervals [5].

Measurement and evaluation

Economics has also tested itself. Camerer and colleagues replicated all 18 between-subject laboratory experiments published in the American Economic Review and the Quarterly Journal of Economics from 2011 to 2014. They found a significant effect in the same direction as the original for 11 of them (61%), and on average the replicated effect was 66% of the original size. Peers trading in a prediction market before the replications expected a higher rate, 75%, though the difference is not statistically significant [7].

New data sources raise their own measurement questions. Jean and colleagues combined nighttime maps with high-resolution daytime satellite images and used machine learning to estimate household consumption and assets, quantities that are hard to measure in poorer countries; the data they used were publicly available and nonproprietary [8]. Whether such estimates hold across countries and years is a question each new application has to answer for itself.

Where economics meets AI

Generative AI is the latest addition. Korinek describes dozens of use cases for large language models across ideation, writing, background research, data analysis, coding and mathematical derivations, and rates the capabilities from experimental to highly useful [9]. That grading is itself a useful reminder: tools differ in maturity, and each needs the same scrutiny applied to any other estimator.

The common thread is that better prediction does not remove the need for design. The questions economists most want answered, such as what a policy changed, still depend on credible causal reasoning, and methods that combine learning with design are the field's current attempt to deliver it [1][4][5].

Where the field is still arguing

Open questions in the field

  • How can flexible machine-learning models be used for causal questions without biasing the answer?

    Naively plugging machine-learning estimates into standard formulas can cause heavy bias in the quantity of interest, which the double/debiased approach is designed to remove [4].

  • How can researchers estimate who benefits from a policy or treatment, not only the average effect?

    Causal forests were developed to estimate heterogeneous treatment effects and to give valid confidence intervals for them [5].

  • How reliable are published economic experiments?

    A replication of 18 laboratory experiments from two top economics journals found a significant effect in the original direction for 11 of them (61%), and replicated effects averaged 66% of the original size [7].

  • Can satellite imagery and machine learning measure welfare where surveys are scarce, and how accurate is it?

    One study combined nighttime lights with daytime satellite images to estimate household consumption and assets, which are hard to measure in poorer countries [8].

Explainer

How economics checked 18 of its own experiments.

Camerer and colleagues repeated all 18 between-subject laboratory experiments published in the American Economic Review and the Quarterly Journal of Economics from 2011 to 2014, each with a plan posted in advance and at least 90% power to detect the original effect[7].

How researchers work on these questions

Common methods

  • Supervised learning for prediction

    Regression and classification methods that estimate a function from data, useful where the task is prediction rather than parameter estimation [2][1].

  • Double/debiased machine learning

    Combines machine-learning estimates of nuisance quantities with orthogonal scores and cross-fitting to estimate a low-dimensional parameter [4].

  • Causal forests

    An extension of random forests for estimating heterogeneous treatment effects [5].

  • Research design

    Random assignment and other design-based strategies that improve the credibility of empirical work [6].

Keep the boundary visible

What the evidence does not yet show

  • Machine learning revolves around prediction, while many economic applications revolve around parameter estimation, so naive use risks misinterpretation [2].
  • Not every published experiment replicates; in one set of 18, 11 showed a significant effect in the original direction [7].
  • Large-language-model tools range from experimental to highly useful depending on the task [9].

Terms used on this page

Glossary

Causal inference
Methods for estimating the effect of one factor on an outcome rather than just their association.
Heterogeneous treatment effect
An effect of an intervention that differs across groups or individuals.
Nuisance parameter
A quantity that must be estimated along the way but is not the one of interest.
Credibility revolution
The shift in empirical microeconomics toward research design as the main source of credible evidence.
